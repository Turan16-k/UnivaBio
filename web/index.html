LittleHeart

Hear the heart problems that hide in children.

LittleHeart listens to stethoscope recordings from the four heart valve areas and flags children whose heart sounds contain a murmur, so a health worker knows who needs an echocardiogram. The AI runs on this device; no recording is uploaded.

LittleHeart is a research prototype for screening support, not a medical device and not a diagnosis. It was trained on children in north-east Brazil recorded with one stethoscope model; accuracy on other devices and populations is unknown. A child who seems unwell should see a clinician whatever the result.

How the AI works

  1. Spectrogram. Each recording is turned into a log-mel spectrogram: a picture of which pitches (25–1000 Hz) are loud every 16 ms.
  2. 4-second windows. The picture is cut into overlapping 4-second windows, about 4–8 heartbeats each.
  3. CNN. A small convolutional neural network (4 layers, about 61,000 weights) scores each window: murmur, no murmur, or unclear.
  4. Per valve. Window scores are averaged for each recording.
  5. Per child. If any valve looks like a murmur, the child is referred. If none does but a recording is unclear, it asks for a re-recording.

How we tested it

  • Split by child, not recording. All recordings of a child are either in training or in testing, never both.
  • Cross-validation. Every child is tested once by a model that never heard them.
  • Thresholds chosen on separate data. Decision cut-offs come from a validation split inside the training folds.
  • Baseline. A logistic regression on average spectrum shows how much the CNN adds.
  • Untouched demo cases. The 10 real cases on the first tab were picked at random and removed before any training. LittleHeart agrees with the cardiologist on 7. The other 3 are children without a murmur that it asks to re-record; it misses none of the 4 murmurs.

Safety check: is it really a heartbeat?

The CNN has only ever heard heart sounds, so it could give a confident but meaningless answer for speech or noise. Before trusting it, LittleHeart checks that the low-pitched loudness rises and falls with a regular rhythm between 40 and 220 beats per minute. Recordings that fail are never called "murmur" or "no murmur"; the health worker is asked to record again.

Known limits

  • Misses about 1 in 4 murmurs in testing
  • Trained on one region (Brazil) and one device
  • Cross-validation on public data is not the challenge's hidden test set
  • Plain phone microphones are much noisier than stethoscopes
  • Rhythmic sounds that are not heartbeats (e.g. a metronome) can pass the safety check
  • Validation scores above do not include the heartbeat check, which asks for a re-recording of about 4% of real recordings

Why listen to children's hearts?

Congenital heart disease is the most common birth defect, and rheumatic heart disease still damages the hearts of millions of children and young people. Both are often first noticed as a murmur, but echocardiography and cardiologists are scarce where these diseases are most common.

What is a murmur?

A whooshing sound between the normal "lub-dub" heart sounds, caused by turbulent blood flow. Many murmurs in children are innocent, but some come from holes in the heart or damaged valves.

Who is LittleHeart for?

Community health workers, school screening programmes and primary care clinics that have a stethoscope but no cardiologist, and need to decide which children to send for an echocardiogram.

Why AI?

Hearing a murmur takes years of practice, and even trained listeners disagree. An AI that has heard thousands of recordings can give every health worker a consistent second opinion.

The four sites

Each valve is best heard at one spot: aortic and pulmonary in the 2nd rib space either side of the breastbone, tricuspid at its lower left edge, and mitral at the apex of the heart.

Recording tips

Use a quiet room, keep the child calm and still, press the stethoscope firmly on bare skin, and record at least 10 seconds per site. Crying and talking make recordings unclear.

What happens next?

A "refer" result means the child should have an echocardiogram, the ultrasound test that shows the heart's structure. A "no murmur" result does not rule out heart disease.
